### 1. How does P2P networking differ from client/server networking?

Client/server is basically a hierarchy: one machine (the server) keeps all the files and resources, and everyone else just asks it for what they need. The clients are dependent — they can't really do anything without the server being up and responsive. P2P throws that hierarchy out. Every machine on the network is both a client and a server at once, so peers trade files directly with each other, no middleman required. That's what makes P2P decentralized — and, at least in theory, tougher to kill, since there's no single point of failure that takes the whole thing down if it goes offline.

### 2. Why is P2P a potential money-saver for organizations?

The math is pretty simple: instead of one company shelling out for a beefy server, huge storage, and enough bandwidth to handle every user at once, P2P spreads that burden across all the peers. Each machine on the network chips in a bit of its own storage, processing power, and bandwidth, so nobody's footing the whole bill. That's a big part of why P2P shows up in things like large file distribution, distributed backups, or volunteer computing projects — it lets an organization get by with a lot less infrastructure than a traditional setup would need.

### 3. What are some illegal uses of P2P?

Piracy is the obvious one — music, movies, TV, software, e-books, all changing hands without the copyright holder's permission. That's essentially what took Napster down. But it goes beyond that: P2P networks have been used to sneak malware onto people's machines disguised as normal downloads, to distribute child exploitation material, and to pass around stolen data like leaked databases or cracked software keys. Since the traffic goes peer-to-peer instead of through a server a company can watch, it's long been a go-to method for this kind of thing.

### 4. What are some legal uses of P2P?

There's a legitimate side too, and it's a big one. BitTorrent gets used all the time for distributing large, perfectly legal files — Linux distros, big game or software updates — because splitting the load across many peers beats making one server handle everyone. Early Skype ran its VoIP calls over a P2P architecture. Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies are built on P2P networks, where a crowd of nodes verifies transactions instead of one central authority signing off. And projects like SETI@home borrowed spare processing cycles from volunteers' computers to chew through data collectively.

### 5. Why does P2P allow anonymity — and is that a good thing?

With no central server logging who's doing what, it's genuinely hard to trace activity back to a specific user. Files often bounce through several peers before landing at their destination, which muddies the trail even further. Whether that's good or bad kind of depends on who's using it. It can be a real privacy win — people living under restrictive governments can share information or organize without being watched. But that same anonymity is exactly what makes piracy, malware distribution, and illegal file sharing so hard to stamp out, which is a genuine problem for content creators and law enforcement alike.

### 6. How can a government "control" or censor the Web?

There are a few levers governments can pull. They can lean on ISPs operating in their country to block specific sites or IP addresses. They can filter what shows up in search results so banned content just doesn't surface. They can control DNS so certain domains refuse to resolve. Or they can require licenses for ISPs and tech companies to operate, which gives them a stick to threaten companies with if they don't comply. China's Great Firewall is the textbook example — blocking foreign sites, monitoring traffic. And in the most extreme cases, governments have just cut internet access entirely during unrest, to choke off communication and organizing.

### 7. What would happen to e-commerce if the Web split by country?

It would be a nightmare. Right now one of the Web's biggest strengths is that a small business in one country can reach customers anywhere with barely any friction. Fragment that into a separate Web per country, and suddenly companies need different sites, different payment systems, maybe even different technical standards for every market they want into. Cross-border shopping gets way harder — and that's before you even factor in shipping or currency issues. The cost of doing business internationally would spike, the whole "global marketplace" effect would basically evaporate, and it would favor the big players who can afford to build out separate systems everywhere, while smaller businesses got boxed into their home country's Web.

### 8. What are chatterbots, and why would a firm use them?

Chatterbots — chatbots — are programs built to simulate conversation with a person, whether by text or voice. Some are simple and rule-based, just following scripts; others are AI-driven and can parse natural language and respond more flexibly. Firms lean on them mostly for customer service: answering FAQs, walking someone through a purchase, troubleshooting a basic problem, all without needing a human on the other end at 2am. That saves on labor costs and lets a business field way more customer interactions at once, while customers still get an instant response instead of sitting in a queue.

### 9. What are vReps, and why would a business use them?

vReps — virtual representatives — are animated, human-like avatars businesses put on their sites to interact with visitors, kind of like a virtual salesperson. They can guide someone around the site, answer questions, suggest products, or walk a customer through a process in a way that feels more personal than a wall of text. Businesses like them because they make the shopping experience feel closer to talking to an actual person, which builds trust and keeps people engaged longer — without the expense of hiring more staff.
